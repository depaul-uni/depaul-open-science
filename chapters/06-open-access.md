# PART 1️⃣: The Open Access Landscape

## 1.1 The Evolution of Open Access: From Idealism to Infrastructure

### Historical Context: The Serials Crisis and the Birth of a Movement

The open access movement emerged in response to the **serials crisis** of the 1980s-90s, when journal subscription prices increased at rates far exceeding inflation. Between 1986 and 2005, the average cost of journals rose by 302%, forcing libraries to cancel subscriptions and restricting access to publicly-funded research.

> 📜
>
> **Key Milestones in the Open Access Movement:**
>
> - **1991**: arXiv founded by Paul Ginsparg, pioneering preprint distribution for physics
> - **2001**: Public Library of Science (PLOS) founded, advocating for open access to scientific literature
> - **2002**: Budapest Open Access Initiative (BOAI) defines open access and proposes two strategies (self-archiving and open access journals)
> - **2003**: Berlin Declaration on Open Access establishes principles for cultural heritage and scientific knowledge
> - **2008**: NIH Public Access Policy requires NIH-funded research to be deposited in PubMed Central within 12 months
> - **2013**: bioRxiv launches, bringing preprints to life sciences
> - **2025**: Updated OSTP memo requires immediate public access to federally funded research with no embargo

### Peter Suber's Open Access Timeline

Peter Suber, now Open Access advisor at Harvard Library ([Peter Suber | Harvard Library](https://library.harvard.edu/staff/peter-suber)), maintains the definitive [Open Access Timeline](https://oad.simmons.edu/oadwiki/Timeline) documenting the movement's evolution through 2020. This resource tracks policy developments, major declarations, and infrastructure milestones that shaped modern scholarly communication.

For another perspective, see also the [History of the Open Access Movement](https://open-access.network/en/information-on-open-access/history-of-the-open-access-movement) from the Open Access Network.

### The Economic Model Shift: From Subscription to APC

The transition from subscription-based publishing to open access fundamentally changed who pays for scholarly communication. Under the traditional model, **readers** (via individual or institutional subscriptions) bore costs. Under author-pays open access, **researchers and their institutions/funders** pay Article Processing Charges (APCs) to make research freely available.

> This shift has profound implications for equity and access, as we'll explore in Section 1.3.
>

## 1.2 Open Access Models: Understanding Your Options

> 🥇
>
> ### Gold Open Access
>
> **Definition**: Research is published in a fully open access journal where all articles are immediately and permanently free to read, download, and (depending on license) reuse.
>
> **Characteristics:**
>
> - Immediate open access upon publication
> - Typically funded by APCs paid by authors or their institutions
> - Journal indexed in Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ) if legitimate
> - May use CC-BY or other Creative Commons licenses
>
> **Examples**: PLOS ONE, BMC series journals, eLife, Nature Communications (hybrid), Scientific Reports
>
> **APC Range**: $1,000–$5,000+ depending on publisher and journal prestige

> 🌿
>
> ### Green Open Access (Self-Archiving)
>
> **Definition**: Authors self-archive a version of their article in an institutional or subject repository, making it freely available alongside or after publication in a subscription journal.
>
> **Key Concepts:**
>
> - **Pre-print**: Version submitted to journal before peer review
> - **Post-print (Accepted Manuscript)**: Version accepted after peer review but before publisher's typesetting
> - **Version of Record (VOR)**: Final published version with journal formatting and DOI
>
> **Publisher Policies Vary Widely:**
>
> - Some allow immediate deposit of accepted manuscript
> - Most impose **embargo periods** (6-24 months) before public access
> - Some permit only pre-prints, not post-prints
> - SHERPA RoMEO database catalogs these policies (see Section 2.2)
>
> **Common Repositories:**
>
> - **Institutional**: University repositories (e.g., ***Digital Commons@DePaul*** (formerly Via Sapientiae))
> - **Subject**: PubMed Central (biomedical), arXiv (physics/math), SSRN (social sciences)
> - **Generalist**: Zenodo, Figshare, OSF

> 💎
>
> ### Diamond/Platinum Open Access
>
> **Definition**: Open access journals that do not charge APCs to authors or subscription fees to readers. Funding comes from institutions, scholarly societies, or government grants.
>
> **Characteristics:**
>
> - No financial barriers for authors OR readers
> - Often community-governed and non-profit
> - Supported by institutional consortia, libraries, or foundations
> - Most equitable model but limited scalability
>
> **Examples**: *Discrete Analysis*, *Scipost*, many society journals in Latin America (SciELO network), *Logical Methods in Computer Science*

> ⚠️
>
> ### Bronze Open Access
>
> **Definition**: Articles freely readable on publisher website but without an explicit open license, often temporarily.
>
> **Caution**: This model lacks transparency and sustainability. Articles may:
>
> - Become paywalled later
> - Lack reuse permissions
> - Not be archived in repositories
> - Be removed from public access without notice
>
> **Not recommended** as a deliberate open access strategy.

> 🔀
>
> ### Hybrid Open Access
>
> **Definition**: Subscription journals that offer an open access option for individual articles in exchange for an APC, while maintaining subscription access for other articles.
>
> **Controversies:**
>
> - **Double-dipping**: Publishers collect both subscription fees AND APCs
> - More expensive than pure Gold OA (APCs often $3,000-$5,000)
> - Criticized by open access advocates for profiting from both revenue streams
>
> **When to Use:** When required by funder mandates and no suitable Gold OA or Green route exists.

## 1.3 The Economics of Open Access: APCs, Equity, and the Publisher Oligopoly

### Article Processing Charges: The Financial Reality

**Current APC Landscape (2025-2026):**

- **Low-tier**: $500–$1,500 (PLOS ONE, BMC journals, MDPI)
- **Mid-tier**: $2,000–$3,500 (many society journals, Royal Society)
- **High-tier**: $3,500–$11,000+ (Nature Communications $5,790; Cell Press $9,900; Nature/Science for-profit hybrids)

### Global APC Spending: Follow the Money

A 2023 analysis of APC spending revealed striking geographic and disciplinary disparities:

**Top 25 Countries by APC Expenditures (2015-2018)**:

1. United States: ~$200 million annually
2. China: ~$140 million
3. United Kingdom: ~$120 million
4. Germany: ~$60 million
5. Netherlands: ~$40 million

**By Field of Science** (505,555 publications analyzed):

| **Field** | **APC Expenditure** |
| --- | --- |
| Natural Sciences | $450.5 million |
| Medical/Health Sciences | $423.1 million |
| Engineering/Technology | $104.3 million |
| Social Sciences | $44.5 million |
| Agricultural Sciences | $25.3 million |
| Humanities | $8.3 million |

### The Big Five Academic Publishers: Market Concentration

Five publishers (Elsevier, Springer Nature, Wiley, Taylor & Francis, SAGE) control the majority of scholarly publishing and have successfully pivoted to open access as a **highly profitable business model**.

**APC Revenue for Major Publishers (annual estimates):**

| **Publisher** | **Annual APC Revenue** |
| --- | --- |
| Springer Nature | $589–648 million |
| Elsevier | $221–231 million |
| Wiley | $141 million |
| Taylor & Francis | $77–85 million |
| SAGE | $32 million |

**Total estimated APC revenue**: Over $1 billion annually across the "Big Five"

**Profit Margins**: Despite claims that APCs merely cover costs, publishers maintain profit margins of 23-37%, comparable to subscription-era profitability. A detailed analysis published in an MDPI journal  -  Pollock, D., & Michael, A. (2024). [Same Coin, Different Value: A Multi-Year Comparative Analysis of Financial Performance of Open Access and Legacy Publishers](https://www.mdpi.com/2304-6775/12/1/6). *Publications*, 12(1), 6  -  found that open access publishing remains **highly lucrative** for commercial publishers.

### Equity Concerns: Who Bears the Burden?

> **Critical Question**: "As open access develops, one of the challenges is to ensure that it reduces inequity and does not compound existing problems and inequalities."  - BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine
>

> 🚨
>
> **The APC Barrier**: While open access removes reader barriers, it creates **author barriers**:
>
> - Researchers in low-income countries cannot afford $3,000-$5,000 APCs
> - Early-career researchers without grants struggle to publish
> - Scholars in unfunded disciplines (humanities, social sciences) face disproportionate burdens
> - **Concentrates publishing** among well-funded researchers and wealthy institutions

**Strategies to Reduce APC Burden:**

- **Institutional negotiations**: Read-and-publish (transformative) agreements
- **APC waivers**: Many publishers offer need-based waivers (but not well-publicized)
- **Review/editorial waivers**: Guest editors and frequent reviewers may receive credits
- **Diamond OA**: Choose APC-free journals when available
- **Green OA**: Self-archive to meet mandates without paying APCs

## 1.4 Federal Funding Mandates: Compliance Requirements

### NIH Public Access Policy (2008–Present)

Since 2008, the National Institutes of Health requires:

- All peer-reviewed articles arising from NIH funding deposited in **PubMed Central**
- **12-month embargo** allowed (but now moving to immediate access under 2025 policy)
- Applies to research funded in whole or in part by NIH
- Non-compliance can affect future funding

**Compliance Pathway**: Green OA (deposit accepted manuscript) or Gold OA (if journal deposits on behalf of author)

### NSF Public Access Requirements

The National Science Foundation requires:

- Articles deposited in **NSF-PAR** (Public Access Repository) or other approved repository
- Data management plans with data sharing provisions
- Immediate access goal (following 2025 OSTP memo)

> 🏛️
>
> ### 2025 OSTP Memo: Immediate Public Access
>
> In August 2025, the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy updated guidance requiring **immediate public access to federally funded research with no embargo period**. This represents a major shift from the previous 12-month embargo.
>
> **Implications:**
>
> - Eliminates embargo periods previously allowed
> - Requires manuscripts or published versions available immediately upon publication
> - Applies to all federal agencies funding research
> - Forces researchers and institutions to develop compliance strategies (Gold OA or Green OA with immediate deposit)

### Institutional Compliance Support

Many universities now offer:

- **Open access funds**: Pools of money to cover APCs
- **Library support**: Deposit services for Green OA compliance
- **Author addenda**: Tools to retain rights for self-archiving
- **Transformative agreements**: Institutional read-and-publish deals with publishers

**Check with your institution**: DePaul's library maintains a [Grant Funding Public Access Mandates guide](https://libguides.depaul.edu/openaccess/fundermandates).

# PART 2️⃣: Navigating Publisher Policies and Licensing

## 2.1 Creative Commons Licensing: Choosing the Right License

### What is Creative Commons?

**Creative Commons (CC)** provides standardized, machine-readable licenses that allow authors to retain copyright while granting specific reuse permissions. CC licenses solve the problem of "all rights reserved" default copyright by enabling "some rights reserved".

### The Six CC Licenses (Most to Least Permissive)

> ✅
>
> **1. CC BY (Attribution)**  -  *Most common in academic publishing*
>
> **Permissions**: Others can distribute, remix, adapt, and build upon the work, even commercially, as long as they credit the original creator.
>
> **Use in Research**: Recommended by funders (NIH, Wellcome Trust, Gates Foundation) and major publishers (PLOS, BMC, Springer Nature OA).
>
> **When to Use**: Default for most scholarly articles when you want maximum reuse and impact.

**2. CC BY-SA (Attribution-ShareAlike)**

**Permissions**: Same as CC BY, but derivative works must use the same license (copyleft).

**Use in Research**: Less common in academic publishing. Useful for educational materials when you want remixes to remain open.

**3. CC BY-NC (Attribution-NonCommercial)**

**Permissions**: Others can distribute, remix, adapt, but not for commercial purposes.

**Caution**: Definition of "commercial" is vague. May prevent inclusion in commercial databases or course packs, use by for-profit companies (even for R&D), and translation services that charge fees.

**When to Use**: When concerned about commercial exploitation, but understand this reduces discoverability and reuse.

**4. CC BY-NC-SA (Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike)**

**Permissions**: NonCommercial use only, and derivatives must use same license.

**Caution**: Most restrictive combination. Limits reuse significantly.

> 🚫
>
> **5. CC BY-ND (Attribution-NoDerivatives)**
>
> **Permissions**: Redistribution allowed, but no modifications.
>
> **Not recommended for research**: Prevents remixing, translation, adaptation - core to scientific progress.
>
> **6. CC BY-NC-ND (Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivatives)**
>
> **Permissions**: Most restrictive CC license - download and share only, no changes, no commercial use.
>
> **Not recommended for research**: Minimal openness; defeats purpose of open access.

> 🌐
>
> ### CC0 (Public Domain Dedication)
>
> Not technically a license, **CC0** waives all rights and places work in the public domain. Recommended for:
>
> - **Data and databases** (removes legal barriers to reuse)
> - **Metadata** (enables machine harvesting)
> - **Facts and measurements** (not copyrightable in many jurisdictions anyway)
>
> **NIH and other funders encourage CC0 for data**.

### How to Choose: The CC License Chooser

Use the [Creative Commons License Chooser](https://chooser-beta.creativecommons.org/) to select an appropriate license by answering:

1. Do you want to allow adaptations of your work?
2. Do you want to allow commercial uses of your work?
3. Do you want to require ShareAlike for adaptations?

**Funder Requirements Override Personal Preference**: Always check grant requirements first. Many funders mandate CC BY.

## 2.2 SHERPA RoMEO: Decoding Publisher Self-Archiving Policies

### What is SHERPA RoMEO?

**SHERPA RoMEO** is a comprehensive database of publisher copyright and self-archiving policies, maintained by Jisc in the UK. It answers: **"Can I deposit my article in a repository, and if so, which version and when?"**

**Website**: [https://v2.sherpa.ac.uk/romeo/](https://v2.sherpa.ac.uk/romeo/)

**See also**: [Jisc Open Policy Finder](https://openpolicyfinder.jisc.ac.uk/)  -  an alternative tool for finding open access policies for journals and publishers.

> 🎨
>
> ### Understanding Publisher Colors (Legacy System, Still Referenced)
>
> Historically, SHERPA RoMEO used a color-coding system (now replaced by more granular data):
>
> - **Green**: Pre-print AND post-print archiving allowed
> - **Blue**: Post-print (accepted manuscript) archiving allowed
> - **Yellow**: Pre-print archiving allowed, but not post-print
> - **White**: Archiving not formally supported
>
> While the current interface provides more detailed information, these categories help conceptualize publisher openness.

### How to Use SHERPA RoMEO

**Step 1**: Search for your target journal by name, ISSN, or publisher

**Step 2**: Review archiving policy summary, which typically specifies:

- **Pre-print policy**: Can you post the submitted version?
- **Post-print policy**: Can you post the accepted manuscript?
- **Publisher's version**: Can you post the final published PDF?
- **Embargo periods**: How long must you wait before posting?
- **Repository requirements**: Where can you post (institutional, subject, personal website)?
- **License restrictions**: What can others do with the deposited version?

**Step 3**: Check **conditions**:

- Must include specific acknowledgment text
- May not post on commercial platforms (e.g., ResearchGate, [Academia.edu](http://Academia.edu))
- May require specific Creative Commons license
- Funder-specific allowances may override standard policies

> 📋
>
> **Example Entry** (Hypothetical):
>
> - **Pre-print**: Author can archive pre-print
> - **Post-print**: Author can archive post-print (accepted manuscript) after **12-month embargo**
> - **Publisher's PDF**: Not permitted
> - **Location**: Non-commercial institutional or subject repositories only
> - **Condition**: Must include DOI link and publisher citation

### When Publisher Policy Conflicts with Funder Mandates

**Funder mandates typically override publisher policies**. If a journal has a 24-month embargo but NIH requires immediate access, the author (or institution) can invoke:

- **Copyright addenda**: Modify publisher agreement before signing
- **Author rights provisions**: Reserve right to deposit in funder-mandated repositories
- **Choose a different journal**: Select one with compatible policies

**Resources:**

- **SPARC Author Addendum**: [https://sparcopen.org/our-work/author-rights/](https://sparcopen.org/our-work/author-rights/)
- **University of California's template**: Allows deposit after 6 months regardless of publisher policy

## 2.3 Avoiding Predatory Publishers: Due Diligence and Red Flags

### What Makes a Publisher "Predatory"?

**Jeffrey Beall** (2012) coined the term **"predatory open access publishing"** to describe publishers that charge APCs without providing legitimate peer review, editorial services, or long-term preservation. These publishers exploit the author-pays model, prioritizing profit over scholarly integrity.

### Beall's List: History and Controversy

**Jeffrey Beall's List** of predatory publishers (2012-2017) became the unofficial industry standard. However, legal threats led to its removal in 2017. Today, an **anonymous continuation** exists ([Predatory Journals List 2025](https://predatoryjournals.com/)), though without Beall's direct oversight.

> **Note**: Some legitimate publishers (MDPI, Frontiers) appear on unofficial predatory lists, highlighting the controversy around these designations.
>

### Evidence-Based Red Flags (Think. Check. Submit.)

The **Think. Check. Submit.** campaign provides a checklist for evaluating journal legitimacy: [https://thinkchecksubmit.org/](https://thinkchecksubmit.org/)

> ✅
>
> **Legitimate Journal Indicators:**
>
> - Journal listed in **Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ)** or major indexing services (Web of Science, Scopus)
> - Clear peer review process described
> - Identifiable editorial board with real, reputable scholars in the field
> - Transparent APC information (no surprise fees)
> - Published by recognized scholarly publisher or society
> - Articles have DOIs from Crossref or DataCite
> - Professional website with institutional affiliation
> - ISSN registered and verifiable

> 🚩
>
> **Predatory Publisher Red Flags:**
>
> - **Unsolicited emails** inviting submissions with flattery ("based on your excellent work...")
> - **Too-fast peer review** (acceptance in days or weeks)
> - **Vague or absent peer review process**
> - **No named editors** or fabricated editorial boards (fake scholars, misused names)
> - **Hidden or confusing APC structure** (surprise charges after acceptance)
> - **Poor website quality**: Grammatical errors, broken links, stock photos
> - **Fake impact factors** (not from Clarivate/JCR)
> - **Broad, unfocused scope** ("International Journal of All Sciences")
> - **Pressure to publish quickly** and pay immediately

### The Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ)

**DOAJ** ([https://doaj.org/](https://doaj.org/)) maintains a **quality-controlled list of 20,000+ legitimate open access journals**. Inclusion requires:

- Transparent peer review
- Clear editorial board
- Open access within 2 months of publication
- Proper licensing (CC BY recommended)
- No embargo periods
- Permanent archiving arrangements
- Quality control standards

**Best Practice**: If a journal claims to be open access, verify it's in DOAJ before submitting.

**Training Resource**: For a structured course on identifying predatory publishers, see [Recognizing and Avoiding Predatory Journals](https://www.toziernanern.de/goto.php?target=crs_37&client_id=TOERN) from TOERN (Thüringer Open Educational Resources Network).

### Case Study: The MDPI Model Controversy

**MDPI** (Multidisciplinary Digital Publishing Institute) uses a model where nearly all issues are "Special Issues," faculty are recruited as unpaid Guest Editors who receive APC waivers for their own papers, and Guest Editors must recruit other authors who pay APCs.

> "When every issue is 'Special', it really diminishes the value of 'Special Issues' in general. It's a predatory practice."
>

**Guidance**: Publish in MDPI **only if** you have an APC waiver, the journal is indexed in your field's major databases, and you've verified recent articles are of acceptable quality.

# PART 3️⃣: Preprints and Strategic Dissemination

## 3.1 The Rise of Preprints: From Physics to Pandemic

### What is a Preprint?

A **preprint** is a complete scientific manuscript shared publicly **before formal peer review**. Preprints accelerate dissemination, enable rapid feedback, and establish precedence.

> **Key Distinction**: Preprints are **not peer-reviewed publications**. They represent the author's un-vetted work.
>

### arXiv: The Original Preprint Server (1991)

**Paul Ginsparg** founded [arXiv.org](http://arXiv.org) in 1991 for physics preprints. Over 30 years later, arXiv hosts:

- **2+ million preprints** in physics, mathematics, computer science, quantitative biology, quantitative finance, statistics, and electrical engineering
- **Moderated submission** (not peer-reviewed, but screened for appropriateness)
- **Permanent preservation** with version control

**Impact**: In fields like high-energy physics, preprints on arXiv are the **primary mode of communication**, often cited before (or instead of) journal publication.

### bioRxiv & medRxiv: Bringing Preprints to Life Sciences

**bioRxiv** (launched 2013) and **medRxiv** (launched 2019) brought preprint culture to biology and medicine. These servers screen submissions for basic scientific content, categorize by subject area, assign DOIs for citability, and track downstream publication in peer-reviewed journals.

> 🦠
>
> **Pandemic Impact**: During COVID-19, bioRxiv and medRxiv became **critical infrastructure** for rapid sharing of research. A 2026 retrospective by the servers' founders noted:
>
> > "The pandemic was a stress test for bioRxiv and medRxiv that demonstrated their value and robustness."
> >
>
> **OpenRxiv**: In 2025, bioRxiv and medRxiv transitioned to a non-profit organization called **openRxiv** to ensure long-term sustainability.

### ChemRxiv (2017) and Chemistry Preprints

**ChemRxiv** ([https://chemrxiv.org](https://chemrxiv.org)) launched in 2017 for chemistry preprints. Unlike bioRxiv (which has some screening), ChemRxiv accepts all chemistry submissions without moderation, is operated by American Chemical Society, and allows posting before or after peer review.

**Cultural Challenge**: Chemistry has been slower to adopt preprints due to concerns about **patent priority** (public disclosure before patent filing), **scooping** by competitors, and **data integrity** (sharing synthesis details before peer verification).

### Discipline-Specific Preprint Servers (2015–Present)

**OSF Preprints** ([https://osf.io/preprints/](https://osf.io/preprints/)) serves as an aggregator and platform for community preprint servers:

- **PsyArXiv** (psychology)
- **SocArXiv** (social sciences)
- **EdArXiv** (education research)
- **LawArXiv** (legal scholarship)
- **MediArXiv** (media, film, communication studies)
- **MetaArXiv** (meta-research, research on research)
- **EarthArXiv** (earth sciences)
- **BioHackrXiv** (biohackathons)
- **PaleorXiv** (paleontology)
- **Thesis Commons** (theses and dissertations)

### Major Preprint Servers: Comparison Table

| **Server** | **Discipline** | **Founded** | **Moderation** | **DOI Assignment** | **Indexing** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

## 3.2 Benefits of Preprints: Speed, Feedback, and Visibility

> ⚡
>
> ### Accelerated Dissemination
>
> Traditional peer review takes **6-18 months** from submission to publication. Preprints are posted within days, allowing:
>
> - **Rapid communication** during emergencies (pandemics, natural disasters)
> - **Timely findings** that inform policy and practice
> - **Faster scientific progress** by sharing methods before final publication

**Key Reading**: Majumder, M. S., & Mandl, K. D. (2020). [The evolution, benefits, and challenges of preprints and their interaction with journals](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3000959). *PLOS Biology*, 18(6), e3000959.

### Community Feedback Before Publication

Preprints enable:

- **Open peer review** via comments, PubPeer, or Twitter (X) discussions
- **Methodological improvements** before formal submission
- **Collaboration opportunities** from interested researchers
- **Course correction** if major flaws identified early

**Best Practice**: Share preprints with your professional network and invite feedback before journal submission.

### Establishing Intellectual Priority

Preprints with DOIs are **time-stamped, citable records** that establish:

- **Precedence** for discoveries (important in competitive fields)
- **Protection against scooping** (you have public proof of your ideas)
- **Credit for your work** even if journal publication is delayed or rejected

### Increased Visibility and Citations

Research shows preprints:

- Appear in Google Scholar, increasing discoverability
- Can be cited in grant proposals and CVs as "under review"
- Often receive **early citations** that boost eventual article impact
- Enable researchers without journal access to read your work

**Key studies on OA citation benefits:**

- Langham-Putrow, A., et al. (2021). [Open access research outputs receive more diverse citations](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11192-021-04033-7). *Scientometrics*, 126, 5227–5249.
- Atchison, A., & Bull, J. (2015). [Benefits of Using Open Access: Citation Analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3103/S0005105515040025). *Automatic Documentation and Mathematical Linguistics*, 49(4), 131–137.
- Vicente-Saez, R., et al. (2024). [The academic impact of Open Science: A scoping review](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsos.231656). *Royal Society Open Science*, 11(1), 231656.

### Version Control and Iterative Science

Preprint servers allow **posting updated versions** with explanations of changes:

- **Version 1**: Initial submission
- **Version 2**: Revised after peer review (can note "accepted at Journal X")
- **Version 3**: Final published version (with DOI link to journal)

This creates a **transparent record** of how the work evolved.

## 3.3 Challenges and Criticisms of Preprints

> ⚠️
>
> ### Public Misunderstanding of "Not Peer-Reviewed"
>
> **The Problem**: Non-scientists (journalists, policymakers, general public) may not distinguish preprints from peer-reviewed publications.
>
> **Evidence**: A 2025 study found that **most news consumers don't know what preprints are** or that they lack peer review. Headlines from preprints during COVID-19 created confusion and misinformation. See also the *Science* news coverage: [Preprints often make news. Many people don't know what they are](https://www.science.org/content/article/preprints-often-make-news-many-people-don-t-know-what-they-are) (*Science*, AAAS).
>
> **Solution Strategies:**
>
> - Preprint servers must **prominently display** "This article is a preprint and has not been peer-reviewed"
> - Journals and preprint servers collaborate on **clearer labeling**
> - Science communication training emphasizes responsible preprint discussion
> - **Cite responsibly**: Always note preprint status when referencing

### Fear of Scooping

**Concern**: Posting ideas publicly before publication allows competitors to publish first or steal ideas.

**Reality**: Scooping fears are often **overestimated**. Evidence shows:

- **Time-stamped DOIs** establish precedence
- Replicating complex research takes time; simply reading a preprint doesn't enable instant replication
- Most researchers are **collaborative, not predatory**

> "I've been hesitant to do pre-prints because things move slow in my research and I've scooped and been scooped before."  - Kyle Grice
>

Grice later co-authored a preprint that was subsequently published: Moltved, K. A., et al. (2022). [A Redox Transmetalation Step in Nickel-Catalyzed C-C Coupling Reactions](https://chemrxiv.org/engage/chemrxiv/article-details/62b8dc1d8ccee037d0d3c8a2). *ChemRxiv*.

**Guidance**: In fast-moving, highly competitive fields, consider posting preprints **after journal acceptance but before publication** to minimize scooping risk while gaining visibility.

### Abuse by Paper Mills and Citation Manipulation

**Problem**: Preprint servers have been exploited for paper mills (fake or plagiarized research posted for profit), self-citation schemes, and low-quality content.

**Evidence**: A February 2025 *Scientific Reports* paper documented **citation manipulation through citation mills and preprint servers**.

**Mitigation Strategies:**

- Preprint servers implement **screening for obvious fraud**
- Journals develop **policies for citing preprints**
- Researchers apply **critical evaluation** even to preprints

### Should You Cite Preprints?

**The Debate:**

- **Pro**: Preprints are citable, time-stamped works that should receive credit
- **Con**: Citing un-peer-reviewed work legitimizes findings that may be flawed

**Emerging Consensus**: Cite preprints when necessary (e.g., if findings are critical and not yet published), always note preprint status in the citation, update citations when the peer-reviewed version appears, and apply extra scrutiny when evaluating preprint claims.

> 📝
>
> **Example Citation**:
>
> > Smith, J., Doe, A., & Lee, K. (2025). Novel findings in X. *bioRxiv*. [https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.01.01.123456](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.01.01.123456) [Preprint, not peer-reviewed]
> >

> "Talked to someone who failed to cite our preprint that showed an AlphaFold model with lots of experimental validation when he submitted [a] cryo-EM paper of exact same system in Nature with no bio, data 6 months later. He said as a matter of policy 'I don't cite preprints.' Unethical."
>

**Bottom Line**: Cite preprints with transparency and update citations post-publication.

## 3.4 Publisher Policies Toward Preprints

### The Ingelfinger Rule (Historical Context)

In the 1960s, *NEJM* editor Franz Ingelfinger established a rule: **no prior public disclosure** of research findings before journal publication. This was intended to maintain exclusive first publication, prevent sensationalized media coverage, and ensure peer review before public dissemination.

**Modern Relaxation**: Most major journals now **allow preprints** while still prohibiting press releases or media coverage before peer review.

### Current Publisher Stances on Preprints

> 👍
>
> **Preprint-Friendly Publishers:**
>
> - **PLOS**: Actively encourages preprints; integrated with bioRxiv
> - **eLife**: Posts preprints of all submissions
> - **Nature Portfolio**: Allows preprints; may consider preprint reviews during journal review
> - **BMJ**: Allows preprints for most journals
> - **American Chemical Society**: Encourages ChemRxiv for chemistry papers

**Restrictive Publishers:**

- **Cell Press**: Caution against preprints for scoopable topics but generally allow
- **Some medical journals**: May have restrictions due to clinical implications

**Best Practice**: Always check **author instructions** for your target journal regarding preprint policies before posting.

## 3.5 Selecting the Right Preprint Server for Your Discipline

### Decision Framework: Which Server?

**Step 1**: Does a **discipline-specific** server exist?

- **Yes** → Use it (better discoverability within your community)
- **No** → Proceed to Step 2

**Step 2**: Is your work **multidisciplinary** or doesn't fit existing categories?

- Use **Zenodo** (CERN-hosted, permanent, multidisciplinary, excellent for data/code too)
- Or **OSF Preprints** (integrates with OSF project management)

**Step 3**: Does your institution have an **institutional repository**?

- Some universities allow preprint deposits (check policies)
- May count toward institutional open access mandates

**Step 4**: Consider **funder preferences**

- Some funders specify approved repositories (e.g., Europe PMC for Wellcome Trust)

### Posting Workflow: Best Practices

> 📤
>
> **Before Posting:**
>
> 1. **Check target journal policy** (confirm preprints allowed)
> 2. **Remove identifying information** if submitting for blind peer review (post after acceptance instead)
> 3. **Verify co-author approval** (all authors must agree to public posting)
> 4. **Proofread thoroughly** (preprints are permanent; versions can be updated but errors persist)
>
> **At Posting:**
>
> 1. **Choose appropriate license** (CC-BY recommended)
> 2. **Provide clear title and abstract** (will be indexed by Google Scholar)
> 3. **Add keywords and categories** for discoverability
> 4. **Include contact information** for feedback
>
> **After Posting:**
>
> 1. **Share on social media and email lists** (maximize visibility)
> 2. **Monitor for feedback** (comments, emails, Twitter/X mentions)
> 3. **Update with peer-reviewed version** once published (with note linking to journal DOI)
> 4. **Track metrics** (views, downloads, citations via Google Scholar Alerts)

### Special Considerations by Discipline

**Biomedical/Clinical Research**: Use **medRxiv** (screened for medical relevance). Be cautious with **clinical implications** (preprints may influence practice before peer review). Follow **reporting guidelines** (CONSORT, etc.) even for preprints.

**Chemistry**: Use **ChemRxiv** (ACS-operated). **Patent considerations**: Consult legal counsel if patent filing is pending (public disclosure can affect patentability).

**Social Sciences**: Use **SocArXiv** or **SSRN** (though SSRN now Elsevier-owned). Consider **OSF Preprints** (integrates with OSF project workflow).

**Humanities**: Preprint culture less established. Consider **Humanities Commons** or **institutional repositories**. Focus on **book chapters, conference papers** as preliminary outputs.

# PART 4️⃣: Measuring Impact Beyond Citations

## 4.1 Traditional Citation Metrics: Limitations and Biases

### The H-Index and Journal Impact Factor

**Journal Impact Factor (JIF)**:

- Average citations per article in a journal over 2 years
- **Limitations**: Skewed by highly-cited review articles; manipulable; not applicable to individual articles

**H-Index**:

- A researcher has index *h* if *h* of their papers have at least *h* citations
- **Limitations**: Favors long careers; penalizes early-career researchers; discipline-dependent

> ⚠️
>
> **Problems with Citation-Only Metrics:**
>
> - **Long lag time**: Citations accumulate slowly (years after publication)
> - **Publication bias**: Closed-access articles receive fewer citations from low-resource institutions
> - **Self-citation**: Manipulable
> - **Discipline disparities**: Humanities cite less than biomedical sciences
> - **Ignore broader impact**: Policy influence, teaching use, public engagement

## 4.2 Altmetrics: Capturing Broader Research Impact

### What are Altmetrics?

**Altmetrics** (alternative metrics) track **online attention and engagement** with research outputs beyond traditional citations. They capture social media mentions (Twitter/X, Facebook, LinkedIn), news coverage (mainstream media, science blogs), policy document citations (government reports, clinical guidelines), post-publication peer review (PubPeer, PubMed Commons), reference manager saves (Mendeley, Zotero), and Wikipedia citations.

### Major Altmetrics Platforms

> 🍩
>
> #### [Altmetric.com](http://Altmetric.com) (Digital Science)
>
> **Features:**
>
> - **Altmetric Attention Score**: Weighted aggregate of mentions across sources
> - **Donut visualization**: Color-coded by source type (news=red, Twitter=blue, blogs=purple, policy docs=yellow)
> - Tracks 3 million+ articles across 29,000+ journals
> - Free badges for publishers and institutional repositories
>
> **Use Cases**: Identify research with **policy impact**, track **media coverage**, demonstrate **public engagement** in grant reports and tenure files.

> 📊
>
> #### PlumX Metrics (Elsevier)
>
> **Features:**
>
> - Five categories: **Usage, Captures, Mentions, Social Media, Citations**
> - Tracks diverse outputs: **articles, books, datasets, code, videos, presentations**
> - Integrated with **Scopus** and **institutional repositories**
>
> **Advantage**: Broader than Altmetric (includes more platforms and artifact types).

> 👤
>
> #### ImpactStory (Profiles for Researchers)
>
> **Features:**
>
> - Create a **researcher profile** aggregating all outputs and impacts
> - Automatically updates from **ORCID, GitHub, Zenodo, Figshare**
> - Displays **altmetrics for all artifact types** (articles, datasets, software, posters)
>
> **Use Cases**: Build a comprehensive research portfolio for tenure/promotion packages.

### Interpreting Altmetrics: What They Mean (and Don't Mean)

**High Altmetric Score Indicates:** Attention and engagement (not necessarily quality or validity), potential broader impact outside academia, and communication success (if intentionally promoted).

**Altmetrics DO NOT:** Replace peer review or scholarly citations, indicate scientific rigor or correctness, provide complete picture (many researchers don't use Twitter/X), or account for gaming (authors can artificially inflate scores).

**Best Practice**: Use altmetrics as **complementary evidence** alongside traditional metrics, peer review, and qualitative impact narratives.

# PART 5️⃣: Strategic Dissemination and Practical Implementation

## 5.1 Building a Dissemination Strategy: Pre-Planning for Maximum Impact

### The Four Phases of Research Dissemination

> 1️⃣
>
> **Phase 1: Pre-Registration (Covered in October Meeting)**
>
> Register study protocol on OSF, AsPredicted, or [ClinicalTrials.gov](http://ClinicalTrials.gov). Establishes **transparency and precedence**.

> 2️⃣
>
> **Phase 2: Pre-Publication Sharing (Preprints)**
>
> Post preprint **before or immediately after journal submission**. Benefits: Rapid dissemination, community feedback, citable record.

> 3️⃣
>
> **Phase 3: Publication (Journal Article)**
>
> Select journal based on **audience, open access model, cost, prestige**. Ensure compliance with **funder mandates**. Choose appropriate **license** (CC BY recommended).

> 4️⃣
>
> **Phase 4: Post-Publication Amplification**
>
> **Social media promotion** (Twitter/X threads, LinkedIn posts), **press releases** (if university communications office supports), **conference presentations** and posters, and **blog posts** and science communication articles.

### Journal Selection Decision Tree

**Start Here**: What are my constraints?

**Question 1**: Does my funder mandate immediate open access?

- **Yes** → Requires Gold OA or Green with no embargo → Filter journals by funder-compliant policies
- **No** → Proceed to Question 2

**Question 2**: Can I afford APCs ($0-$11,000)?

- **Yes** → Consider Gold OA journals
- **No** → Focus on **Diamond OA** (free to authors and readers) or **Green OA** (subscription journals allowing self-archiving)

**Question 3**: Is there an institutional read-and-publish agreement?

- **Yes** → Check library's list of negotiated deals (APCs covered or discounted)
- **No** → Proceed to Question 4

**Question 4**: What is my disciplinary audience?

- **Broad multidisciplinary**: PLOS ONE, Scientific Reports
- **Specific subfield**: Society journals, specialty OA journals

**Question 5**: How important is prestige (Impact Factor)?

- **Critical** (tenure/promotion): Consider hybrid OA in high-IF journals
- **Less critical**: Prioritize fully open access options

### Managing APC Costs: Strategies for Researchers

> 💰
>
> **Strategy 1: Apply for Waivers**
>
> Many publishers offer **need-based waivers** for authors in low-income countries (automatic or by request), graduate students and early-career researchers, and unfunded research.
>
> **How to Ask**: Email journal editor explaining circumstances; waivers often granted but under-publicized.
>
> **Strategy 2: Use Institutional Open Access Funds**
>
> Many universities maintain **OA publication funds**. Apply before submission (some require pre-approval). Often requires corresponding author at institution.
>
> **Strategy 3: Leverage Editorial/Review Waivers**
>
> **Guest editors** of special issues often receive APC waivers (be cautious of exploitative models like MDPI's). **Frequent reviewers** may receive credits or discounts. **Society members** may get reduced APCs.
>
> **Strategy 4: Include APCs in Grant Budgets**
>
> **NSF, NIH, European funders** allow APCs as direct costs. Budget $2,000-$5,000 per article in grant applications.
>
> **Strategy 5: Choose Diamond OA or Green OA**
>
> **Diamond OA**: Free to authors and readers (check DOAJ for options). **Green OA**: Publish in subscription journal, self-archive accepted manuscript after embargo.

## Key Takeaways

> 📌
>
> **Open access is not monolithic**  -  Gold, Green, Diamond, and Platinum models have different costs, benefits, and equity implications. Understand your options before committing to a journal.

> 📌
>
> **APCs range from $0 to $11,000+**  -  Don't assume you must pay. Seek waivers, use institutional funds, negotiate as Guest Editors/reviewers, or choose Diamond OA and Green OA pathways.

> 📌
>
> **SHERPA RoMEO is essential**  -  Publisher policies vary wildly. Always check self-archiving rights before signing copyright agreements. Consider author addenda to retain rights.

> 📌
>
> **Predatory publishers exploit the author-pays model**  -  Use DOAJ, Think. Check. Submit., and reputation checks to avoid scams. If a journal isn't indexed and has vague peer review, be skeptical.

> 📌
>
> **Preprints accelerate science but require responsible use**  -  Post preprints for rapid dissemination and feedback, but always note preprint status when citing. Update citations post-publication.

> 📌
>
> **Federal mandates now require immediate open access**  -  The 2025 OSTP memo eliminates embargo periods. Plan compliance through Gold OA or Green OA with immediate deposit.

> 📌
>
> **Creative Commons licenses enable reuse**  -  Default to CC-BY for maximum impact unless funder or journal requires otherwise. Avoid NC and ND restrictions when possible.

> 📌
>
> **Altmetrics complement (not replace) citations**  -  Track social media, news, and policy citations using [Altmetric.com](http://Altmetric.com) and PlumX. Demonstrate broader impact in tenure files and grant reports.

> 📌
>
> **Strategic dissemination multiplies impact**  -  Combine preprints, open access publication, social media, press releases, and repository deposits for maximum visibility.

> 📌
>
> **Your institution can help**  -  Use library services for APC funding, repository deposits, copyright guidance, and transformative agreement navigation.
