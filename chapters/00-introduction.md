# Chapter 0 - Introduction

## Why This Guide Exists

Modern research is in the middle of a quiet revolution. Funders ask grant applicants to pre-register their hypotheses. Journals require analysis code with submitted manuscripts. Tenure committees increasingly ask candidates to document the reproducibility of their published work. Students arrive with the expectation that data, code, and protocols will be openly shared.

For most working researchers, none of this was part of their training. The tools, the platforms, the conventions, and the expectations have all shifted in the past decade, and the official guidance from any single institution is patchy, fragmented, or out of date.

This guide is the antidote. It is a concrete, opinionated, discipline-aware playbook for adopting the open-science practices that contemporary research now demands. It was written by faculty across six DePaul units who spent the 2025-26 academic year together: meeting monthly, presenting on practices, debating disciplinary differences, and trying these tools in their own research.

## What's Inside

The guide follows the open-science research lifecycle:

1. **Foundations** - what the reproducibility crisis actually is, and what we mean by *open*
2. **Pre-registration** - committing to your design before the data arrive
3. **Open data** - sharing data responsibly and discoverably
4. **Reproducible analysis** - making sure tomorrow's reader can rerun what you did
5. **Transparent reporting** - communicating methods and results without hidden flexibility
6. **Open access publishing** - getting your work to readers without paywalls
7. **Version control with GitHub** - the connective tissue across all of the above
8. **Open materials** - sharing protocols, stimuli, and code so others can build on your work
9. **Integration** - sustaining open-science practice past any single project

Each chapter is adapted from the live record of an SLC meeting. The substantive content - principles, tools, references, discipline-specific notes - all survives. The chapters were edited for chapter format, not rewritten from scratch.

## How to Use the Guide

- **Linear read.** End-to-end takes about three hours. Recommended if open science is new to you.
- **Practice-first.** Skip to the chapter on the practice you need this month. Each is self-contained.
- **As a reference.** The [reading list](../resources/reading-list.md), [tools and platforms catalog](../resources/tools-and-platforms.md), and [glossary](../resources/glossary.md) consolidate everything cited across the chapters.

## A Note on Limits

Open science is not a panacea. Pre-registration does not magically prevent p-hacking. Open data does not automatically protect human subjects. Code on GitHub does not automatically reproduce six years from now. Each practice has costs, edge cases, and implementation traps. We try to be honest about them throughout, and where we don't have the answer, we say so.

We also recognize that disciplines vary in their open-science maturity, in their ethical constraints around data sharing, and in their professional incentives. A chemistry lab and a qualitative criminology project face genuinely different problems. The guide tries to address that range, not flatten it.
