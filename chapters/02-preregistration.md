## 🎯 Meeting Objectives

By the end of this session, participants will:

- **Understand** the evolution and rationale behind pre-registration as a tool for research rigor
- **Navigate** pre-registration platforms and select appropriate templates for diverse research designs
- **Develop** comprehensive study protocols with transparent methodology documentation
- **Apply** discipline-specific pre-registration practices to ongoing research projects
- **Distinguish** between exploratory and confirmatory research approaches
- **Implement** transparent deviation management and reporting strategies

## Essential Supplementary Resources Beyond the Core Readings

Beyond the foundational papers already provided, several discipline-specific guides and empirical studies offer practical implementation guidance. **The Registration Continuum in Clinical Science** (Benning et al., 2019) introduces a uniquely practical framework distinguishing preregistration (before data collection), coregistration (during collection), and postregistration, addressing clinical research's unique challenges including recruitment difficulties, complex phenotypes, and extensive measurement batteries. This comprehensive guide provides detailed decision trees for clinical populations rather than rigid single plans, filling a critical gap for researchers working with clinical samples.

For translational researchers, **The case for pre-registered confirmatory pharmacological pre-clinical studies** (Hånell et al., 2018) demonstrates why distinguishing exploratory from confirmatory pre-clinical work matters. The authors analyzed 30 randomly selected traumatic brain injury pharmacology studies, finding substantial design discrepancies compared to Phase III trials: minimal power calculations (3 of 30), limited blinding (22 of 30), no pre-determined outcomes, and selection bias toward positive results. Their introduction of author network analysis as a tool to assess independent replication addresses the persistent gap where promising pre-clinical findings fail in clinical trials. They recommend registration at [preclinicaltrials.eu](http://preclinicaltrials.eu) with specific requirements for power calculations, matching age and sex to clinical populations, and pre-specifying primary outcomes.

**Opportunities and challenges for Registered Reports in ecology and evolution** (Nakagawa & O'Dea, 2022, Nature Communications) addresses field-specific obstacles including long data collection periods, unpredictable field conditions, and time pressures on short-term contracts. The authors clearly distinguish Registered Reports from simple pre-registration and emphasize that **observational research can be just as suitable** as experimental work. Their evidence that null results increased from 10% to 60% in Registered Reports demonstrates the format's effectiveness in reducing publication bias. Solutions include pilot studies, clear deviation documentation, and distinguishing predictions from post-hoc discoveries.

J-PAL's **Trial Registration Resource Guide** (updated July 2023) serves as the definitive practical guide for economists and social scientists conducting randomized controlled trials. It provides step-by-step instructions for the AEA RCT Registry, explains donor and journal requirements, and addresses registration considerations specific to field experiments in development economics where intervention delivery may be uncertain. The guide's practical focus on registration on behalf of principal investigators and downloading registry data for analysis makes it immediately useful.

The most recent empirical evaluation comes from **Preregistration of Psychology Meta-Analyses** (Sandoval-Lentisco et al., 2025). Of 1,403 eligible meta-analyses published in 2021, only 27% were pre-registered, exposing 77% to bias from data-dependent decisions. Among 100 pre-registered meta-analyses examined in detail, key PRISMA-P items were often omitted (median coverage: 13 of 17 items), with frequent omissions including publication bias assessment, multiplicity handling, heterogeneity assessment, and meta-analysis model specification. The authors recommend using PRISMA-P tools, simulating data to understand decisions, and creating pre-specified decision rules for different scenarios - guidance applicable to meta-analyses in any field.

Two organizational resources round out the essentials. The **Center for Open Science Template Selection Guide** (2024) helps navigate the 11+ templates available on OSF, explaining terminology differences, template purposes (comprehensive OSF Preregistration, streamlined AsPredicted, Generalized Systematic Review, Qualitative Preregistration, Secondary Data Analysis), and practical considerations like embargo periods and deviation handling. The **UK Reproducibility Network** provides the most comprehensive institutional-level implementation model, with Train-the-Trainer workshops, evaluation materials, primers, and institutional implementation guides developed with UNESCO and multiple national networks. Their 2024 survey of 2,500+ research-active staff documented current practices and barriers, making UKRN's resources invaluable for institutional adoption efforts.

## Pre-registration Templates for Diverse Research Needs

Researchers face a spectrum of template options ranging from minimal 8-question formats to comprehensive regulatory-compliant registrations. Understanding which template matches your research design saves time and ensures appropriate rigor.

### Templates by Complexity Level

**For quick, straightforward registrations**, AsPredicted offers 8 essential questions covering data collection status, hypotheses, variables, conditions, analyses, outliers, and sample size. Completion takes 30-60 minutes, generating a time-stamped PDF. The platform's simplicity comes with limitations: registrations remain private by default (problematic for file-drawer transparency), lack comprehensive guidance, and provide no file storage integration. Research shows AsPredicted registrations may be less informative than OSF alternatives, yet the format suits pilot studies, early-stage exploratory work, and researchers finding comprehensive templates burdensome.

**OSF Preregistration** represents the most widely applicable comprehensive option, with approximately 30 questions across 9 sections covering study information, research questions, sampling plan, variables, design plan, analysis plan, and more. Available in multiple formats (Google Docs, R Markdown, LaTeX) with extensive guidance for each question, it formerly served as the "Prereg Challenge" template. The template works best for hypothesis-testing research requiring detailed documentation, particularly studies undergoing rigorous review or replication attempts. While the learning curve is steeper than AsPredicted, OSF provides project management integration, 5GB file storage, version control, and collaboration features, with all registrations eventually becoming public after a maximum 4-year embargo.

### Specialized Templates for Specific Research Types

**Secondary data analysis** demands special consideration given the double-dipping risk. The Secondary Data Preregistration template (van den Akker et al., 2021) builds on OSF Prereg while adding critical questions about researchers' prior exposure to data. This template requires explicit statements about familiarity with variables, what researchers already know about datasets, and how prior knowledge will be managed. The accompanying tutorial paper provides worked examples for analyzing publicly available datasets like Add Health, NLSY, or national surveys where researchers might have seen summary statistics or published findings.

**Qualitative research** increasingly recognizes pre-registration's value despite methodological differences from quantitative work. The Qualitative Preregistration template accommodates emergent designs while documenting methodological rigor through clear specifications of research questions, theoretical frameworks, sampling strategies, data collection procedures, and analysis approaches. Key adaptations include acknowledging iterative development, documenting decision-making processes, and distinguishing predetermined structure from emergent themes. The template addresses common concerns that preregistration constrains qualitative flexibility by focusing on process transparency rather than outcome prediction.

**Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** benefit from PROSPERO registration (for health-related reviews) or OSF's Generalized Systematic Review Registration. These templates emphasize search strategies, inclusion/exclusion criteria, data extraction protocols, and planned analyses. The PRISMA-P checklist (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols) provides 17 essential items including eligibility criteria, information sources, risk of bias assessment, and synthesis methods. Recent evidence shows pre-registered systematic reviews demonstrate higher methodological quality and more comprehensive reporting than non-registered reviews.

**Replication studies** use specialized templates that document original study details, planned deviations, and justifications for methodological changes. The Replication Recipe template guides researchers through specifying what aspects require exact replication versus which allow modification. Critical elements include power analysis based on original effect sizes, materials procurement documentation, and planned sensitivity analyses. Transparent documentation of differences from original protocols enables meta-scientific evaluation of replication success and helps distinguish failed replications from methodological artifacts.

**Clinical trials** must comply with regulatory requirements through platforms like [ClinicalTrials.gov](http://ClinicalTrials.gov) (US), the EU Clinical Trials Register, or the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform. These registrations mandate comprehensive documentation including primary/secondary outcomes, participant eligibility criteria, intervention details, and planned statistical analyses. The CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines provide reporting standards that should inform registration content. Failure to register clinical trials before enrollment can result in publication rejection, funding ineligibility, and regulatory penalties.

### Emerging and Specialized Templates

**Computational modeling and simulation studies** face unique pre-registration challenges around model specifications, parameter choices, and robustness checks. The Simulation Studies Preregistration Template (developed by Siepe, Bartoš, & Pawel, 2024) provides structure for documenting data-generating mechanisms, performance metrics, and competitor methods. The template addresses common degrees of freedom in simulation research, from how researchers generate data to which methods they compare. This specialized template proves particularly valuable for later-stage research providing guidance to applied researchers.

**Observational and correlational research** benefits from pre-registration despite lacking experimental manipulation. Templates for observational studies emphasize research questions, variable definitions, confound adjustment strategies, and sensitivity analyses. The Berkeley Initiative for Transparency in the Social Sciences provides observational study templates addressing causal inference, measurement selection, and model specification. Dewitte et al. (2021) demonstrate how non-experimental psychological research can be pre-registered, arguing that transparency benefits apply equally to correlational and experimental work.

## When to Preregister, What to Include, and the Crucial Role of Pilot Data

Timing proves critical for effective pre-registration. Register immediately before data collection begins - after methodology finalization but before accessing any outcome data. This timing maximizes planning benefits while establishing clear temporal boundaries. For multi-phase projects, consider separate registrations for each distinct phase rather than a single comprehensive document that becomes unwieldy. Studies with rolling enrollment should register before the first participant rather than waiting for complete sample assembly.

**What to include in comprehensive preregistrations:**

Research questions should be stated with sufficient precision to falsify, avoiding vague objectives like "explore relationships" in favor of specific predictions. Hypotheses require directional predictions with explicit links to theoretical frameworks - not just "X will affect Y" but "X will increase Y because of mechanism Z, consistent with Theory A." This specificity enables clear evaluation of whether results support, refute, or fail to address research questions.

Sampling plans must justify sample size through power analyses, practical constraints, or resource availability. Specify target populations, recruitment methods, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and stopping rules. For studies using sequential analysis, document decision rules for early termination or sample size modification. Address missing data handling strategies and participant attrition expectations before encountering such issues.

Variable definitions require operational specifications for all constructs. Document measurement instruments, scoring procedures, and any data transformations. For validated scales, specify which version and any modifications. Novel measures require clear development procedures and reliability assessment plans. Control variables, covariates, and moderators should be identified with justifications for their inclusion.

Analysis plans represent pre-registration's core value, requiring comprehensive documentation of statistical models, assumptions, and decision rules. Specify primary analyses that address main hypotheses and secondary/exploratory analyses that examine additional questions. Detail how violations of statistical assumptions will be handled - predetermined transformation procedures, alternative analyses, or sensitivity checks. Document multiple comparison corrections, planned contrasts, and adjustment for confounds.

Deviations from pre-registered plans occur frequently and are entirely acceptable when transparently documented. The Transparent Changes template provides a structured format for recording and justifying modifications. Common justifications include: unexpected data characteristics (e.g., non-normal distributions requiring alternative analyses), recruitment challenges necessitating adjusted sample sizes, methodological improvements discovered during data collection, or reviewer suggestions during peer review. What matters is transparent reporting of what changed and why, not perfect adherence to original plans.

### The Critical Role of Pilot Data

Pilot studies serve as pre-registration's essential complement, addressing concerns about rigid protocols while improving study quality. Well-designed pilots test feasibility, refine procedures, and inform power analyses without compromising the main study's confirmatory status. The key distinction: pilot data may inform but must not overlap with confirmatory data.

**Legitimate pilot data uses** include testing recruitment strategies, refining measurement instruments, assessing manipulation effectiveness, identifying potential confounds, and estimating effect sizes for power calculations. Pilots reveal practical obstacles - participant comprehension difficulties, time constraint issues, or measurement problems - that would be costly to discover mid-study. Effect size estimates from pilots inform realistic power analyses rather than relying on optimistic published estimates that often overestimate true effects.

**Preventing pilot data contamination** requires careful boundaries. Pilot participants must be excluded from the confirmatory sample or analyzed separately. If pilots use the same population as the main study, document how independence is maintained. Small pilots (n < 30) typically provide unstable effect size estimates, so use confidence intervals around pilot effects for power calculations rather than point estimates. Consider internal pilots where early participants serve dual purposes - if preregistered appropriately, initial data can inform adaptive decisions while maintaining overall study integrity.

**Sequential and adaptive designs** represent sophisticated approaches allowing data-dependent decisions within pre-registered frameworks. These designs specify decision rules in advance: at what sample sizes will interim analyses occur, what constitutes sufficient evidence for early stopping, and how will Type I error be controlled across multiple looks at data. The key is specifying these rules before any data analysis, making the entire decision tree transparent. Lakens et al. (2021) provide tutorials on group sequential designs that balance efficiency with rigor.

## Practical Implementation: From Platform Selection to Protocol Development

### Platform Selection and Account Setup

Choosing the right platform depends on discipline, study type, and institutional requirements. **OSF Registries** offers the broadest applicability with comprehensive features, multiple template options, and integration with OSF's project management tools. Create an OSF account at [osf.io](http://osf.io), verify your email, and explore example preregistrations before starting your own. The platform's version control means you can save drafts privately before finalizing registration.

[**AsPredicted.org**](http://AsPredicted.org) suits researchers wanting minimal bureaucracy. The 8-question format focuses on essential decisions without extensive documentation. However, registrations default to private visibility, requiring manual public sharing. No account creation needed - just provide an email address for the time-stamped PDF. The platform's simplicity comes at the cost of reduced guidance and collaborative features.

**Discipline-specific registries** may be required or preferred. Clinical trials must use [ClinicalTrials.gov](http://ClinicalTrials.gov) or equivalent regulatory registries. Economists should use the AEA RCT Registry ([socialscienceregistry.org](http://socialscienceregistry.org)). Education researchers can use the Registry for Educational and Social Studies (REES, [rees.rice.edu](http://rees.rice.edu)). Ecology and evolution researchers may prefer OSF or discipline-specific options like the Open Science Framework for Ecology and Evolution. Psychology researchers have multiple options: OSF, AsPredicted, or PsychArchives.

### Writing Effective Pre-registrations

Begin with the big picture before diving into details. Draft research questions in everyday language, then progressively add precision. Ask colleagues unfamiliar with your work to identify ambiguities - if they can't understand your hypotheses and methods, reviewers will struggle too. The first draft should be comprehensive but imperfect; iteration improves clarity.

**Hypothesis specification** requires more precision than typical manuscript introductions. Instead of "We hypothesize that X affects Y," specify direction ("X will increase Y"), magnitude ("by approximately 0.3 standard deviations"), and conditions ("among participants with characteristic Z"). Link each hypothesis explicitly to theoretical predictions, making clear why specific patterns would support or refute your theory. This precision enables unambiguous outcome interpretation.

**Methods sections** in pre-registrations should be comprehensive enough that someone else could replicate your study without additional information. Include participant recruitment specifics: where, how, and when recruitment occurs; screening procedures; and compensation details. Document materials in detail or reference openly available protocols. For experiments, specify randomization procedures, blinding methods, and manipulation check plans.

**Analysis plans** benefit from concrete examples using simulated data. Create a synthetic dataset matching your expected structure, then write and test your analysis code. This process reveals ambiguities and edge cases: How will you handle participants who miss sessions? What defines an outlier? How will you treat repeated measures with missing timepoints? Document decisions before encountering real data that might bias choices.

Statistical power analysis deserves particular attention. Justify your sample size through formal power calculations (showing target effect size, alpha, power, and sample size relationships), resource constraints (acknowledging limitations while maximizing sample given resources), or precision analysis (determining sample size needed for desired confidence interval width). Avoid convenient but unjustified rules like "n = 30 per group." If power is limited, acknowledge this and discuss implications for interpretation.

### Quality Assurance Through Pre-registration Review

Solicit feedback before finalizing registration. Share drafts with colleagues, asking them to identify ambiguities or questionable research practices you might have missed. Specific questions to ask reviewers: Are the hypotheses falsifiable? Do the analyses directly test the stated hypotheses? Are there potential confounds we haven't addressed? Could the sampling plan be improved? This peer review before registration improves study quality more than post-hoc manuscript review.

The Preregistration Checklist (adapted from Bakker et al., 2020) provides systematic quality evaluation. Essential items include: research questions stated as clear, falsifiable predictions; variables operationally defined; sample size justified; analysis plans specified with enough detail to implement; and handling of potential issues (missing data, outliers, assumption violations) documented.

Consider the "What Would You Do?" test: present your pre-registration to colleagues with various hypothetical scenarios ("What if your manipulation check fails?" "What if recruitment goes viral and you collect 5,000 instead of 500 participants?"). Their ability to determine appropriate actions based solely on your pre-registration reveals whether you've provided sufficient detail and decision rules.

## Discipline-Specific Challenges and Solutions

Pre-registration's principles transcend disciplines, but implementation varies significantly across fields. Understanding these variations helps researchers apply pre-registration appropriately rather than rigidly imposing methods designed for other contexts.

### Psychology and Social Sciences

Psychology pioneered pre-registration adoption following replication crisis revelations. Challenges include participant recruitment variability, sensitive measures requiring IRB approval, and exploratory research traditions. Solutions: use sequential designs allowing flexible stopping rules, pre-register IRB-approved protocols after approval but before data collection, and clearly distinguish confirmatory from exploratory analyses in pre-registrations.

Social psychology particularly struggles with context-sensitive effects and small samples. Address this through: precise theoretical specifications identifying boundary conditions, pre-registered equivalence tests to demonstrate null effects meaningfully, and multisite collaborations enabling adequately powered studies. The Psychological Science Accelerator demonstrates how distributed collaboration overcomes individual laboratory limitations.

### Biomedical and Clinical Research

Clinical research faces regulatory requirements making pre-registration mandatory rather than optional. Challenges include long study durations, multiple endpoints, and adaptive designs responding to interim data. Solutions: use established trial registries ([ClinicalTrials.gov](http://ClinicalTrials.gov)), pre-specify primary outcomes distinguished from exploratory secondary outcomes, and document adaptive design decision rules before trial commencement.

Pre-clinical research increasingly recognizes pre-registration value despite resistance from some quarters. Hånell et al.'s (2018) analysis of pre-clinical pharmacology reveals widespread absence of basic design elements: power calculations, pre-specified outcomes, and independent replication. The [preclinicaltrials.eu](http://preclinicaltrials.eu) registry addresses these gaps, though adoption remains limited. Key barriers include perceived competitive disadvantages and concerns about publishing negative results, both addressable through institutional culture change.

### Ecology and Evolution

Field biology faces unique challenges: long data collection periods, unpredictable conditions, and observational study predominance. Nakagawa & O'Dea (2022) emphasize that observational research benefits from pre-registration as much as experimental work. Solutions: clearly document what's planned versus what must be determined in the field, use deviation documentation protocols for unexpected circumstances, and distinguish predictions (pre-specified patterns) from post-hoc explanations.

Long-term ecological studies spanning years or decades require special consideration. Pre-register overarching frameworks and hypotheses while acknowledging that specific measurements or analyses might adapt to emerging opportunities. The key is transparent documentation of what was planned versus what emerged, enabling appropriate interpretation of confirmatory versus exploratory findings.

### Economics and Social Policy

RCTs in development economics often pre-register through the AEA RCT Registry, with many journals requiring pre-registration for publication consideration. Challenges include uncertain intervention delivery in field settings, multiple stakeholder interests, and ethical obligations sometimes requiring protocol modifications. Solutions: pre-register with realistic assumptions about implementation fidelity, document anticipated challenges and contingency plans, and use pre-analysis plans distinguishing primary hypotheses from secondary questions.

Observational economics research increasingly adopts pre-registration despite lacking experimental intervention. Hedge et al. (2021) describe how secondary data analysis can be pre-registered by specifying hypotheses, variables, and models before accessing data. This approach works for restricted-access datasets where researchers can document plans before receiving data, though public datasets require more careful handling of prior knowledge.

### Humanities and Qualitative Research

Qualitative research traditions emphasize emergent design and inductive analysis, seeming incompatible with pre-registration. However, qualitative pre-registration focuses on process transparency rather than outcome prediction. Document research questions, theoretical frameworks, sampling strategies, data collection procedures, and analysis approaches while acknowledging that themes and insights will emerge during research.

Qualitative pre-registrations benefit: demonstrate rigor to skeptical reviewers, establish temporal precedence for emerging theoretical contributions, and distinguish systematic methodology from convenience samples or selective reporting. The key adaptation: frame pre-registration as documenting procedures and decision-making processes rather than predicting specific findings.

### Computational and Data Science

Computational research faces questions about whether code development constitutes "data collection" necessitating pre-registration. Simulation studies clearly benefit from pre-registration, as the Simulation Studies template demonstrates. Machine learning research might pre-register model selection criteria, performance metrics, and validation procedures before accessing test data.

Data science working with existing datasets must carefully consider prior knowledge. If you've seen the data or know key patterns, acknowledge this limitation while still pre-registering analyses. The value shifts from preventing data-dependent decisions to constraining analytic flexibility and documenting transparent inference. Hold-out datasets enable confirmatory analysis even when training data were previously explored.

## Interactive Activities for Teaching and Learning Pre-registration

Effective pre-registration education combines conceptual understanding with hands-on practice. These activities suit faculty development workshops, graduate seminars, or research team training.

### Mini Pre-registration Writing Exercise

**Time: 20-30 minutes**

Participants work individually or in pairs to draft mini-preregistrations for ongoing or planned research. Provide simplified templates with 5-7 essential questions: What are your main hypotheses? How will you collect data? What analyses will test your hypotheses? What's your sample size and how did you determine it? How will you handle missing data or outliers?

This exercise reveals common difficulties: vague hypotheses, under-specified analyses, and overlooked methodological details. Sharing drafts in small groups facilitates peer learning as participants see others' approaches and identify gaps in their own thinking. The facilitator circulates, providing targeted feedback on common issues like insufficient precision or problematic analytic flexibility.

### "Find the Holes" Activity

**Time: 15-20 minutes**

Provide participants with example pre-registrations containing deliberate ambiguities or questionable practices. Examples include: "We will analyze the data and report significant findings" (lacks specific analyses), "Sample size will be determined by available participants" (no power justification), or "We hypothesize that X affects Y" (lacks directional prediction).

Working in small groups, participants identify problems and suggest improvements. This activity trains critical evaluation skills applicable to both their own pre-registrations and others'. Discussion reveals common pitfalls: p-hacking opportunities disguised as flexibility, HARKing enablers in vague hypotheses, and analytical degrees of freedom unrecognized by researchers. The activity demonstrates that pre-registration quality matters - poorly specified plans provide minimal protection against bias.

### "What Would You Do?" Scenario Analysis

**Time: 15 minutes**

Present scenarios where researchers face unexpected issues:

- "Your data aren't normally distributed as expected. Your pre-registration specified t-tests. What do you do?"
- "Recruitment went viral and you collected 5,000 participants instead of 500 planned. How do you proceed?"
- "Your manipulation check shows the intervention didn't work as intended, but you already collected all data. What do you report?"
- "A reviewer suggests an analysis you didn't pre-register that produces significant results. Do you include it?"

Pairs discuss what should be reported and how to handle deviations transparently. This builds understanding that pre-registration isn't rigid constraint but transparent framework. Appropriate responses include: documenting deviations with justifications, conducting pre-registered analyses even if results are null, supplementing with additional analyses clearly labeled exploratory, and explaining in manuscripts how actual implementation differed from plans.

### Template Selection Activities

**Time: 10 minutes**

Provide brief descriptions of 3-4 research projects from different disciplines:

- A clinical trial testing a new drug for depression with 200 participants
- A secondary analysis of existing survey data examining socioeconomic predictors of health outcomes
- An ethnographic study exploring community responses to policy changes
- A meta-analysis synthesizing findings from 50 studies on a educational intervention
- A simulation study comparing statistical methods' performance under various conditions

Participants match each to appropriate preregistration templates (OSF Prereg, AsPredicted, Qualitative Preregistration, Systematic Review Registration, Simulation Studies Template, etc.) and discuss why certain templates fit certain designs. This activity builds understanding that pre-registration is flexible, with templates adapted to research needs rather than one-size-fits-all.

### Exploratory Versus Confirmatory Analysis Sorting

**Time: 10 minutes**

Provide lists of 10 analysis decisions or research activities for participants to categorize:

- Testing pre-specified hypothesis with pre-determined analysis (confirmatory)
- Analyzing all possible variable combinations to find significant relationships (exploratory)
- Conducting pre-registered equivalence test (confirmatory)
- Discovering unexpected pattern and investigating it further (exploratory)
- Implementing analysis plan specified before data access (confirmatory)
- Transforming variables after seeing their distributions (exploratory)
- Testing predictions derived from theory before data collection (confirmatory)
- Generating hypotheses from observed patterns (exploratory)
- Following pre-registered decision tree for handling outliers (confirmatory)
- Trying multiple analysis approaches to find best-fitting model (exploratory)

Discussion emphasizes that both research modes contribute essential value to science - exploratory work generates hypotheses while confirmatory work tests them. Pre-registration helps distinguish between them, enabling appropriate interpretation and avoiding the "garden of forking paths" where exploratory analyses masquerade as confirmatory tests.

### Live OSF Walkthroughs

**Time: 10-15 minutes**

Demonstrate creating preregistrations with participants following along on their own devices:

1. Create OSF account at [osf.io](http://osf.io) (1-2 minutes)
2. Navigate to OSF Registries
3. Click "New Preregistration"
4. Select template (demonstrate comparing options)
5. Walk through filling out first few questions of OSF Prereg template
6. Demonstrate saving drafts versus finalizing registration
7. Show embargo options and public/private settings
8. Demonstrate finding and viewing example preregistrations

This hands-on component builds technical competence while addressing common concerns about platform complexity. Participants learn that creating accounts and basic navigation is straightforward, reducing barriers to adoption.

## Discussion Prompts that Encourage Critical Thinking

Effective prompts move beyond superficial agreement to genuine engagement with pre-registration's complexities and limitations.

**"When is preregistration most versus least useful for your research?"** encourages reflection on discipline-specific applicability. Participants might note that hypothesis-testing experiments benefit greatly while early-stage exploratory work may not. This prompts discussion of the confirmatory-exploratory distinction and how pre-registration serves different functions in different research stages.

**"A colleague says preregistration will 'kill serendipity' - how would you respond?"** addresses common misconceptions. Strong responses note that pre-registration doesn't prevent exploratory analysis or unexpected discoveries - it simply requires labeling them as such. Serendipitous findings remain valuable and publishable when reported transparently rather than presented as confirmatory tests.

**"You're halfway through data collection and realize your power analysis was wrong. What do you do?"** explores transparent change documentation. Appropriate responses include: stopping enrollment, revising pre-registration with clear documentation of changes and justifications, analyzing data collected under original plan separately from additional data, or continuing with underpowered study while acknowledging limitations. The key is transparent reporting of what changed and why.

**"How would preregistration change the questions you ask in your research?"** examines impacts on hypothesis formation and theory development. Participants might note that pre-registration encourages more precise theoretical predictions, focuses attention on falsifiable hypotheses, or reveals gaps in methodological planning that need addressing before data collection.

**"What are potential downsides or limitations of preregistration in your field?"** acknowledges legitimate concerns rather than presenting pre-registration as panacea. Field-specific challenges might include: long study durations making protocol modification necessary, complex interventions requiring adaptation, qualitative research's emergent nature, or competitive pressures in fast-moving fields. Discussion should address these concerns seriously while exploring how pre-registration can be adapted.

**"How can preregistration be used as a mentoring tool for students?"** explores pedagogical applications. Pre-registration forces explicit articulation of research plans, revealing gaps in student understanding. The collaborative process of developing pre-registrations provides teaching opportunities around study design, statistical analysis, and research ethics.

**"If you could only preregister one aspect of your next study, what would it be and why?"** prioritizes key preregistration elements. Responses reveal what researchers see as most bias-prone: analysis decisions, outcome selection, sample size determination, or handling of missing data. This discussion highlights that even partial pre-registration (e.g., specifying primary outcomes while remaining flexible about secondary analyses) provides value.

**"How does preregistration relate to other open science practices like open data, materials, and code?"** connects practices and discusses comprehensive transparency. Pre-registration complements other practices: open materials enable replication of pre-registered procedures, open data allows verification of pre-registered analyses, and open code makes computational pre-registrations reproducible.

**"A journal reviewer asks you to remove a null result from your preregistered study. How do you respond?"** addresses editorial challenges and selective reporting. Strong responses politely decline, explaining that pre-registration commits to reporting all planned analyses regardless of outcomes. Reference journal policies supporting registered reports or pre-registration. If necessary, seek alternative journals that value transparency over statistical significance.

**"What would success look like if preregistration became standard in your department?"** envisions cultural change and discusses implementation strategies. Participants might describe: reduced pressure to produce only positive results, increased confidence in published findings, better study design through collaborative pre-registration review, or institutional support for open science practices.

## Case Studies Bringing Concepts to Life

### The Unexpected Subgroup Effect

A researcher preregisters a clinical trial testing a new intervention for anxiety, specifying analyses comparing treatment and control groups. The preregistered analysis shows no overall effect. However, exploring data reveals striking effects for participants over age 50 - the intervention works well for older adults but not younger ones.

**Discussion questions:** Should this subgroup finding be reported? How should it be reported? What follow-up research would be appropriate? How do you distinguish this from post-hoc data mining?

**Key learning points:** Exploratory findings are valuable and reportable when clearly labeled as such. The appropriate response is reporting both the null pre-registered result and the exploratory subgroup finding, explicitly noting that the latter was not hypothesized in advance. Follow-up confirmatory research should preregister the age moderation hypothesis. This case demonstrates that pre-registration doesn't suppress interesting findings - it provides context for their interpretation.

### The Failed Manipulation Check

A researcher preregisters an experiment with careful manipulation checks. After data collection, the manipulation check reveals the intervention didn't work as intended - participants in the "high stress" condition reported similar stress levels to controls. However, the main analyses show significant effects on the dependent variable.

**Discussion questions:** Are the hypothesis tests valid? How should this be reported? What does it mean if manipulation failed but effects occurred? Should the researcher conduct the preregistered analyses despite the failed manipulation?

**Key learning points:** Failed manipulations require transparent reporting rather than burial. The researcher should report: (1) the failed manipulation check, (2) the preregistered results (noting they may not reflect the intended construct), and (3) exploratory explanations for why effects might have occurred despite manipulation failure. This builds understanding that pre-registration enables learning from failures rather than hiding them.

### The Multi-Study Paper

A researcher collects data for three related studies simultaneously, all examining the same theoretical question from different angles. Study 1 is exploratory, examining which variables predict the outcome. Study 2 tests specific hypotheses suggested by Study 1 results. Study 3 replicates Study 2 in a different population.

**Discussion questions:** Should this be one preregistration or three? How should exploratory Study 1 inform confirmatory Study 2? What should be preregistered for Study 3 if Study 2 results are already known? How do you prevent data holdout strategies from creating artificial confirmatory tests?

**Key learning points:** Complex projects benefit from multiple preregistrations rather than single omnibus documents. Study 1 might not be preregistered (or preregistered as explicitly exploratory). Study 2 should be preregistered before conducting its analyses, specifying how Study 1 findings informed hypothesis generation. Study 3 should be preregistered before data collection if possible, or before data analysis if collection overlapped with Study 2 analysis. This case illustrates how pre-registration adapts to realistic research workflows.

### The Archival Data Dilemma

A researcher wants to preregister analysis of existing datasets (e.g., National Health Survey data), but already knows descriptive statistics from published reports. The researcher hasn't analyzed the specific relationships of interest but has general familiarity with the data.

**Discussion questions:** Is preregistration still valuable? What should be disclosed about prior knowledge? How does this differ from preregistering before data collection? What strategies prevent "fishing expeditions" in familiar data?

**Key learning points:** Pre-registration with existing data provides value by constraining analytic flexibility, even if not preventing all data-dependent decisions. The researcher should: (1) explicitly document prior exposure to the data, (2) describe what's already known versus what's being tested, (3) specify analyses completely before examining relevant results, and (4) consider holdout samples or cross-validation approaches. Some journals accept such registrations while others require truly prospective designs.

### Qualitative Research Preregistration

A researcher plans 40 semi-structured interviews examining community responses to policy changes. Interviews will be transcribed and thematically analyzed. Themes will emerge from the data rather than being predetermined.

**Discussion questions:** What can and should be preregistered in qualitative research? How do you preregister emergent design? What's the value of preregistration when outcomes can't be predicted? How is this different from quantitative pre-registration?

**Key learning points:** Qualitative pre-registration focuses on process rather than outcomes. The researcher can preregister: research questions, theoretical frameworks guiding inquiry, sampling strategies and participant characteristics, data collection procedures including interview protocols, and analysis approaches (e.g., thematic analysis using specific coding procedures). The preregistration documents systematic methodology rather than predicted findings, demonstrating rigor to reviewers while maintaining qualitative research's essential characteristics.

## Looking Forward: The Evolving Pre-registration Landscape

The field continues evolving rapidly with new templates emerging for specialized methods, journals expanding Registered Reports options, and institutional support infrastructure developing. Pre-registration represents one tool among many for research rigor - most effective when combined with open materials, open data, and open code.

The goal is transparency enabling scientific self-correction, not perfection in prediction. Both confirmatory and exploratory research contribute essential value to science; pre-registration simply helps distinguish between them, allowing appropriate interpretation of evidence and more accurate accumulation of knowledge across the research community.

As adoption increases, researchers can expect growing institutional support, more discipline-specific guidance, and stronger norms making pre-registration standard practice rather than exceptional effort. The transition requires both individual researcher commitment and systemic changes in how we evaluate, fund, and publish research.
